Our next stop is Shuangxi Park (雙溪公園). Here’s a little pocket of garden scenery about two hundred and twenty-three meters from the stop, where water, stone, and traditional garden design come together.

The park’s name has a local story behind it. Two streams, Waishuangxi and Neishuangxi, flow from north to south and meet in Shilin. That meeting of the waters gives Shuangxi Park its name. So even before we look at the garden, its name tells us something about the landscape around this part of Taipei.

The park sits at the junction of Fulin Road and Zhishan Road, along the way from Shilin toward Yangmingshan, the National Palace Museum, and the two stream areas. It was completed in nineteen seventy-four, with about two hectares open to the public.

Now, here’s something to picture as we approach. The ground is flat, and the park itself is triangular. But within that shape, the design draws on the traditions of Chinese gardens to create a natural-looking landscape of water and rock.

Covered walkways connect the garden’s features. There’s a pavilion in the middle of the pond, waterside structures, a stone cave, and a bridge that follows a winding course. Artificial rocky hills and a waterfall add another layer to the scenery.

What’s worth noticing is how these pieces relate to one another. This isn’t simply a pond with a pavilion beside it. The corridors, bridge, rocks, and water form a connected garden setting, with different features to catch your eye as you look across it.

That combination is part of the park’s character: a sense of modern beauty alongside an older, traditional charm. At this road junction, Shuangxi Park offers a quieter setting within the bustle, with a whole miniature landscape gathered into its two hectares.
