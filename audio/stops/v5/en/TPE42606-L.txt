Wesley Girls’ High School, by Indigenous Peoples Park (衛理女中、原住民文化主題公園), is our next stop. Around here, we have three very different ways to explore culture: through everyday objects, outdoor sculpture, and treasures collected across centuries.

First, just over fifty meters from the stop, is the Shung Ye Museum of Formosan Aborigines (順益台灣原住民博物館). This was Taiwan’s first private museum devoted to Indigenous peoples. Look for its distinctive gray, trapezoid-shaped building, decorated glass windows, and central stone pillar carved with Indigenous motifs.

Inside, four themes introduce the places where different peoples live, their traditional daily lives, their clothing, and their ancestral beliefs. Buildings, crafts, and hunting tools help tell those stories. These are not simply beautiful objects. Together, they offer insight into ways of living closely with mountains and rivers, and honoring ancestral spirits. The museum also occasionally offers related activities and classes.

Nearby, Indigenous Peoples Park takes that cultural encounter outdoors. Patterned paving leads toward the lake and shaded spaces. Sculptures include rice-pounding figures, human and hand motifs, bears, and sika deer, reflecting connections between Indigenous communities and the forests.

There is plenty to notice beside the water, too. Bald cypress trees are reflected in the lake. Their green foliage changes to golden or orange-red tones with the seasons. The broad, gentle paths also accommodate wheelchairs and strollers.

About three hundred and seventy meters from our stop stands the National Palace Museum (國立故宮博物院), completed here in nineteen sixty-five. Its Chinese palace-style architecture houses collections spanning nearly five thousand years.

Here is a lovely detail: there are two versions of its “three treasures.” Scholars point to three monumental Northern Song ink paintings, displayed only rarely. The popular trio is the Jadeite Cabbage, Meat-shaped Stone, and Mao Gong Ding, a bronze cauldron. Together, they bring to mind pickled cabbage and pork hotpot. That playful food connection gives these celebrated objects a wonderfully approachable story.
