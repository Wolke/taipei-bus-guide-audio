Our next stop is National Defense Medical Center (國防醫學中心).

About one hundred and ninety-two meters from this stop is the Taipei City Fire Department’s Fire Safety Museum (臺北市政府消防局防災科學教育館). Its story begins with a serious question: how can people learn about disasters before they have to face one?

An important part of the answer came from Japan. On January seventeenth, nineteen ninety-five, the Hanshin earthquake struck with a magnitude of seven point two on the Richter scale. It killed six thousand four hundred and thirty people and injured more than forty thousand. More than half a million buildings were completely or partly destroyed. The scale of that disaster highlighted the importance of helping ordinary people understand emergencies.

Later that year, on July tenth, the Taipei City Fire Department was reorganized. With disaster education as a priority, it began planning Taiwan’s first disaster prevention science education museum.

Here, the approach goes beyond simply reading about what can go wrong. Computers, electronic systems, and mechanical equipment recreate disaster situations for visitors to experience through hands-on activities. The idea is to make safety knowledge something people can practice, rather than something they only hear about.

The museum introduces the history and characteristics of different disasters. Its educational focus includes fires, floods, earthquakes, and typhoons, along with the knowledge needed to seek safety in an emergency.

What is worth noticing is the connection between these experiences and everyday life. Learning through engaging activities is meant to help people respond more effectively when something unexpected happens.

And the lesson is not meant to end at the museum door. Visitors are encouraged to share what they learn with friends and family. That is the larger purpose behind this nearby museum: practical knowledge passing from one person to another, helping more people understand how to protect themselves.
