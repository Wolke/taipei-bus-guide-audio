MRT Shilin Station, Zhongzheng (捷運士林站中正), is our next stop. This part of Shilin brings together temple history, outdoor sculpture, and the stories behind Taiwan’s traditional pastries.

Nearby is Shilin Shennong Temple (士林神農宮), where a roof once created quite a problem. Originally called Zhilan Temple, it was dedicated to the Earth God. During the Qianlong reign, residents rebuilt it with swallowtail roof decorations reserved for deities of imperial rank. Their grand design expressed deep respect for the Earth God, but it did not follow the architectural rules.

The solution? They made Shennong, a deity of imperial standing, the principal god, protecting the temple from possible demolition. Today, Shennong occupies the center of the upper floor in the main hall, while the Earth God remains below.

Look for the stone dragon columns and carved stone windows. The Shennong figures also tell a story through their faces. Red represents good health from eating cultivated grains. Black recalls his sacrifice in tasting poisonous plants while searching for medicinal herbs.

Fulin Park (福林公園) offers a different kind of visual storytelling. Completed in nineteen ninety-one, this triangular park covers about two point six hectares. Artist Li Zhih-wen created six sculpture groups inspired by the sun, clouds, mountains, waterfalls, water, and wells. Their abstract forms turn pictographic characters into three-dimensional art. Water is cast in bronze; the other five groups use natural stone. Seen close up or from farther away, their shapes create different impressions.

Then there is the Kuo Yuan Ye Museum of Cake and Pastry (郭元益糕餅博物館). Its Shilin branch opened in two thousand two, on the fourth and fifth floors of the company headquarters at the original shop site. Here, pastry history connects with wedding customs, funerals, and celebrations. A hands-on baking space adds another way to explore that heritage. These cakes are more than food; the museum shows their place in life’s important occasions.
