Our next stop is National Palace Museum, Main Building (故宮博物院正館). There is quite a world of art, history, and Indigenous culture around this stop!

The National Palace Museum (國立故宮博物院) was completed here in Waishuangxi in nineteen sixty-five. Its Chinese palace-style architecture sets the scene for a collection of nearly seven hundred thousand objects. These treasures came mainly from the Song, Yuan, Ming, and Qing dynasties, and span almost five thousand years of Chinese history.

Here is a lovely detail: the museum has two different sets of “three treasures.” For art scholars, that means three monumental Northern Song ink paintings. They appear only at intervals of many years, so seeing them is a rare opportunity. The popular trio is the Jadeite Cabbage, the Meat-shaped Stone, and the Mao Gong Ding. Together, they suggest a familiar meal: pickled cabbage and pork hotpot. That playful resemblance gave this trio its nickname.

Exhibitions occupy the first three floors. On the fourth, Sanxitang (三希堂) offers a place for tea and a rest.

Nearby, the Indigenous Peoples Cultural Park (原住民文化主題公園) brings cultural imagery into an open green space. Look for patterned paving leading toward the lake, figures pounding rice, and sculptures of bears and sika deer. These works express connections between Indigenous peoples and the mountain forests. Along the lakeside boardwalk, bald cypress trees reflect in the water. Their foliage shifts from green to gold or orange-red with the seasons.

The Shung Ye Museum of Formosan Aborigines (順益台灣原住民博物館) was Taiwan’s first private museum devoted to Indigenous peoples. Its gray trapezoidal building has painted glass decoration and a central stone pillar covered in Indigenous motifs. Inside, four themes explore communities, traditional life, clothing, and ancestral beliefs. Everyday objects, including hunting tools and crafts, offer another way to understand the cultures represented here: through what people made, wore, used, and believed.
